# ONE · Blind human playtest sheet

**Purpose:** Discover whether a short original-deck shedding game feels clever, tense and worth replaying. Do not explain choices for people while they play. Record observable behavior, not only star ratings.

## Setup

Use exactly the 32 **CORE** cards (2 of each 1–8 × UP/DOWN card), deal 4 to each of two players, and put the remaining 24 cards in the draw stock. All backs identical. The 16 EXTRA cards are removed from this test.

For a three-round set, round 1 leader is chosen by chance; switch leaders in round 2; if tied after round 2, the previous round's **loser** leads round 3. First person to win 2 rounds wins the set.

**Do not run a 3–4-player playtest as though the rules are finalized.** Multi-player termination is not yet validated, even though the engine supports diagnostic bots.

## Group protocol

Recruit around 12 independent two-player groups. If a group plays both a single match and a best-of-three set, alternate testing order across groups. Use another random order to decide who leads the first round. Don't let the designer's coaching bias responses.

- Before play, tell the group only the 3 rules: follow top arrow, same number clears, if stuck take top card and clear remaining pile.
- Observe the first two turns without intervention except to prevent a wrong rule.
- During play, record the exact *moment* a person anticipated and constrained the opponent, rather than just happening to play an extreme arrow.
- Record the winner, number of turns, match duration in **real minutes**, which player started, number of forced pickups, confusion, and how often a player had at least two genuinely distinct options.
- Immediately after the set, ask separately, "Would you rather play ONE again, Shithead, or stop?" Do not describe one option as preferable.
- Ask: "Which move did you seriously consider but reject? Why?" A game isn't deep merely because its UI displays multiple legal cards.
- If the set went to a third game, note whether the rematch felt like a rising rivalry or repetitive filler.

## Test the failure modes

1. **First-player advantage:** Don't only use total set results. Record who started each round and who won. If the set feels fair but single games feel predetermined, the product still needs work.
2. **Stagnation:** Deliberately try sequences that repeatedly transfer extreme 1↓ or 8↑ cards without reducing hand totals.
3. **Incentive quality:** Is there a real decision between restricting the opponent and preserving flexible cards for the end, or does the same rule always win?
4. **Pacing:** Does the last third become exciting once stock runs out? If a player groans about 10 more routine draws, record it.
5. **Teachability:** Do players remember the arrow rule without referring to the written instructions? Can one of them explain it to a newcomer?
6. **Originality:** Would a new player describe it as a distinct game worth keeping, rather than "Shithead with arrows"?

## Decision gate

The project is not a production-ready physical retail game until we have:
- Consistent independent voluntary replay interest
- A demonstrable strategic choice that both players understand
- A tolerable real-world set duration and satisfying finale
- No frequent loops under deliberately competitive play
- A thoroughly reviewed accessible two-person ruleset and real physical proofs
- Distinctive identity, legal originality checks, manufacturing samples and contribution-margin economics

Do not turn these exploratory 12 groups into a statistically definitive sales forecast. If the results are mixed, repeat with a targeted design change, not a wholesale feature pile-on.
